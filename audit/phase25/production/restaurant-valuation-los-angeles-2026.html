How Much Is My LA Restaurant Worth? A 2026 Valuation Guide

8 min readPublished August 27, 2026By Richard Stein, COO
Key Takeaways
  • LA restaurants are valued primarily on a multiple of Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE), not revenue.
  • Lease term, license type, and how dependent the business is on the owner personally all move the multiple.
  • Real estate (if you own the building) is valued and sold separately from the operating business.
  • A defensible valuation takes 24 months of reconciled financials. Guessing from revenue alone undervalues most sellers.
  • Get a valuation before you make any changes to staffing, pricing, or hours: those changes affect the number.

The short answer

Most independently owned Los Angeles restaurants sell for somewhere between 1.5 and 3 times Seller's Discretionary Earnings (SDE), not revenue. Where a specific restaurant lands in that range depends mainly on lease term remaining, license type, how reconciled the books are, and how much the business depends on the current owner personally. A restaurant with a fresh 10-year lease, a Type 47 license, and a GM who isn't going anywhere sits at the high end. A month-to-month lease and an owner who is the entire operation sits at the low end, regardless of how good the food is.

Why LA restaurants are valued on SDE, not revenue

Revenue tells a buyer almost nothing about what they'll actually take home. Seller's Discretionary Earnings starts with net profit, then adds back the owner's compensation, one-time or personal expenses run through the business, and non-cash items like depreciation. What's left is roughly what a new owner-operator could expect to pay themselves in year one. Buyers and their lenders price the deal off that number, so it's the number that matters when you're deciding what to list at.

What counts as an add-back

  • The owner's salary and any family members on payroll who won't stay post-sale.
  • Owner health insurance, vehicle, and cell phone if run through the business.
  • One-time repairs, legal fees, or equipment purchases that won't recur.
  • Interest, depreciation, and amortization.
  • Rent paid above or below fair market value, if the owner also owns the building.

Every add-back needs a paper trail. Buyers and their accountants will ask for it, and an add-back you can't document gets thrown out of the calculation, which lowers your effective SDE and your price.

What moves your multiple up, and what pulls it down

  • Lease term: 5+ years remaining, with options, is worth materially more than 1–2 years left with no renewal language.
  • License type: a Type 47 (full liquor) or a rare CUB-overlay license adds value a beer-and-wine-only concept doesn't have.
  • Owner dependence: if the concept, recipes, and vendor relationships live entirely in the owner's head, buyers discount for the risk of losing all of it at closing.
  • Financial hygiene: 24 months of reconciled POS reports, bank statements, and sales-tax filings that tie together cleanly.
  • Physical condition: a hood, grease trap, and grease interceptor that pass inspection without a rebuild.
  • Trailing trend: a restaurant with flat or growing SDE over the past two years values higher than one on a declining trend, even at the same current SDE.
If you own the real estate

The building and the operating business are two different assets with two different valuation methods. The restaurant business is valued on SDE; the real estate is valued like any commercial property, on comparable sales and cap rate. Don't let a buyer blend the two into one number. Get each appraised separately.

A simplified example

Say a full-service restaurant in West LA nets $180,000 in SDE after add-backs, carries a Type 47 license, and has 6 years left on its lease. A broker might place that restaurant at a 2.2–2.5× multiple, putting the asking price in the $395,000–$450,000 range before inventory and any FF&E adjustments. The same $180,000 SDE with 8 months left on a lease and no license would land closer to 1.2–1.5×, purely because of the risk a buyer is taking on around continuity. The number changes with the deal terms, not just the P&L.

How to get a valuation you can actually rely on

A real valuation starts with 24 months of POS reports, bank deposits, merchant processor statements, and sales-tax filings, reconciled against each other. From there, a broker who works LA restaurant deals specifically, not general small-business sales, applies current local comps for your license type, neighborhood, and lease structure. That local comp knowledge is the difference between a number a buyer's lender will actually underwrite and a number that just sounds good.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions, answered directly.

How much is my restaurant worth in Los Angeles?+

Most independent LA restaurants sell for 1.5–3× Seller's Discretionary Earnings, adjusted for lease term, license type, and how dependent the business is on the current owner. A no-cost valuation call gets you a defensible range specific to your restaurant.

Does revenue or profit matter more for valuation?+

Profit, specifically Seller's Discretionary Earnings, is what buyers and lenders price the deal on. Two restaurants with identical revenue can have very different values if their profit margins, add-backs, or lease terms differ.

Do I need a formal appraisal to sell my restaurant?+

Not always. A broker valuation based on comparable LA sales and your reconciled financials is usually sufficient for a business sale. A formal, certified appraisal is more common when real estate is involved, financing requires one, or there's a legal need (partnership dispute, estate, divorce).

How long is a restaurant valuation good for?+

Treat it as accurate for 3–6 months. Rent increases, a lease renewal (or expiration getting closer), a change in sales trend, or a shift in the local licensing market can all move the number before then.

Will renovating or changing my menu increase my sale price?+

Sometimes, but proceed carefully before a sale: cosmetic renovations rarely pay for themselves in the final multiple, and menu or staffing changes made right before a listing can temporarily distort your trailing financials in ways that make buyers nervous rather than confident.
